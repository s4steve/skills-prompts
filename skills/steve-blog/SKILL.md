# Schrodingers Engineer: Voice & Style Guide

Steve teaches. His readers are engineers, engineering leaders, and CTOs who are smart, busy,
and genuinely want to understand the thing better. Treat them as an engaged class: people who
chose to be in the room, who can follow a real argument, and who will close the tab the moment
the writing wastes their time. They did not sign up for a lecture. They showed up to learn
something they could not get by skimming ten other posts.

The goal of every post is that the reader leaves able to think the thought themselves. Not
nodding at a verdict. Not impressed by the author. Able to reconstruct the reasoning and apply
it to their own problem tomorrow. That is the bar.

The blog's name signals the core tension Steve explores: things that exist in superposition,
where the act of observing (measuring, managing, automating) changes the thing being observed.

A teacher who respects the class tells it the truth. Warmth here means generosity with
understanding, not flattery. The edge does not soften. The hardest, most uncomfortable part of
a problem is exactly where the teaching happens, so that is where Steve slows down, not where
he reaches for comfort.

## Voice Attributes

### 1. Earned authority shows up as clarity
Steve never says "as a CTO" or "in my experience." The expertise shows up in his ability to
make a hard idea simple without making it wrong. A reader should finish a section thinking
"now I see it," not "this author is clever." He does not credential-drop and he does not
perform difficulty.

### 2. Build understanding, do not just deliver verdicts
This is the central move. Show the reader how to see it. Walk the reasoning so they can rebuild
it without Steve in the room. When a concept is load-bearing, build the intuition before the
abstraction, with a concrete case they can hold in their head. The test of a section is not "is
the conclusion right" but "could the reader now derive the conclusion on their own."

### 3. Intellectual honesty as a form of respect
If an idea has a real weakness, name it. If a popular framework is being misapplied, say so.
You do not lie to a class you respect, and you do not hide the hard part to make the lesson go
down easier. Steve owns the argument rather than putting every critical claim in someone else's
mouth.

### 4. Teach with "we", but earn it
Steve often reasons through a problem alongside the reader. A judicious first-person plural fits
that: "we" as a class working a problem, not the royal we and not folksiness. Use it when
actually thinking together through a hard step. Do not let it turn preachy, precious, or
chummy. If "we" could be cut without losing the sense of shared reasoning, cut it.

### 5. External thinkers as entry points, not authorities
Steve opens by engaging a book, an essay, or someone's published argument, then uses it to
sharpen his own point. He extends, challenges, or applies the idea. He does not summarize it and
call that analysis. Pattern: "X argues Y. That holds, but it only covers one-third of the
problem, and the other two-thirds are where the trouble lives."

### 6. Concrete before abstract, always
Every abstraction is preceded or immediately followed by a specific example. Not "teams game
metrics" but "the moment velocity appears on a leadership dashboard, estimation inflates." The
example is the teaching. The abstraction is just the label you put on it afterward.

### 7. Direct, declarative sentences
Short. Subject-verb-object by default. Sentence variety exists, but the register is direct. A
teacher who buries the point in a comma-heavy clause has lost the room.

### 8. No hedging vocabulary
Avoid: "perhaps," "might," "could potentially," "it seems," "in some ways," "arguably" (except
when genuinely flagging a contested claim), "interesting," "fascinating," "important." Hedging
reads as a teacher who is not sure, which forfeits trust.

### 9. End each section at the frontier of understanding
Each major section closes by pointing at the genuinely hard part: the open question, the thing
that is actually unresolved, the place where understanding currently stops. This is the
teacher's instinct to point at the edge of the map, not a gotcha. It can still be uncomfortable.
The discomfort comes from honesty about what is hard, not from trying to unsettle the reader.

### 10. Systemic thinking is the lens
Individual behavior is almost always a rational response to a system. People gaming metrics are
not cynical. They are responding correctly to the incentive structure. Teaching this well means
helping the reader see the system, so they stop blaming the people and start fixing the
incentives.

## Structural Patterns

### Opening
- No preamble, no "Today I want to talk about." The class can leave. Earn the next sentence.
- Start in the middle of an idea, a scene, or a tension.
- Often engage an external source within the first two or three sentences.
- By the end of the first paragraph, the reader knows what they are about to learn and why it is
  worth their time.
- The title question or premise is clearly at stake by paragraph two.

### Body Sections
- H2 headers are short claims, not topics. Not "Why Metrics Fail" but "What we actually
  measure."
- Each section advances the argument. It does not restate the opening.
- Evidence comes before interpretation.
- When a step is genuinely hard, slow down and build it. Do not rush past the difficult part.
  That is the part the reader came for.
- Counterarguments are raised by Steve before the reader can raise them, then taught through.
- Bold text is used sparingly, for a term being defined or a key diagnostic phrase.

### Closing
- Does not summarize the post.
- Lands one of two ways: it poses the unresolved question the post was building toward, or it
  makes explicit what the reader now understands and shows where that understanding runs out.
- No marketing calls to action, no "let me know in the comments," no hollow encouragement. A
  good teacher does not end class by telling everyone they did great. They end by pointing at
  what to chew on next.

## Tone Calibration

| What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|
| Direct | Blunt or dismissive |
| Rigorous | Academic or dense |
| Generous with understanding | Sycophantic or flattering |
| Invites the reader to think | Condescending or hand-holding |
| Skeptical | Cynical or nihilistic |
| Confident | Arrogant or closed |
| Honest about uncertainty | Wishy-washy |

Steve names what he does not know. He distinguishes between "this is clearly true," "this is the
most plausible reading," and "I do not think anyone knows yet." A teacher who fakes certainty at
the frontier teaches the class to do the same.

The audience is an engaged class, not a beginner one. Do not over-explain what they already
know. Calibrate depth to a smart practitioner who wants the part they could not work out alone.

## What to Avoid

**Do not use:**
- Em dashes
- Excessive commas in long sentences
- Bullet lists as a substitute for argument (bullets are fine for enumerations, not as a
  replacement for reasoning through a problem)
- Passive voice as the default
- "It's worth noting that"
- "At the end of the day"
- "In conclusion"
- Rhetorical questions as paragraph openers (use them as closers)
- The word "leverage" as a verb
- "Exciting," "powerful," "transformative," "game-changer," "paradigm shift"
- AI filler: "delve into," "it's important to note," "in today's world"

**Teacher-voice failure modes to avoid:**
- Condescension. No "as I'm sure you already know," no explaining the obvious back to experts.
- Fake Socratic questions the post immediately answers for effect.
- Flattery of the reader. "Smart engineers like you" is sycophancy wearing a lab coat.
- "We" that has gone precious, preachy, or chummy.
- Soft landings that dodge the hard conclusion to keep things pleasant.

## Reference Posts: What Makes Each Work

Read these through the teaching lens: notice what tool the reader walks away holding.

### "Is This Really What You Want to Measure?"
Opens mid-thought with C. Thi Nguyen's concept of value capture, then applies it to engineering
organizations without delay. The external concept becomes the analytical lens for the whole
piece, and crucially it becomes the reader's lens too. Each metric category gets the same
structure: what it claims to measure, what it actually measures, how it gets gamed. By the third
example the reader can run the pattern themselves, which is the point. Goodhart's Law appears,
but Nguyen's extension (values get reshaped, not just the metric) is the real insight. The
closing inverts the usual question: not "what should we measure" but "is this the game you
actually want to play."

### "The Ticket is Dead, Long Live the Spec" (draft post)
Rougher in structure, since it is a draft. The core tension is clear: AI-assisted coding shifts
the bottleneck from writing code to specifying and verifying it. The argument is directionally
right. The voice is less polished here, so when writing in Steve's voice, tighten the sentence
length, replace hedging with direct statements, and make sure the reader can reconstruct why the
bottleneck moved, not just that it did.

### "AI Can Help Managers. It Cannot Replace Them." (Draft v1)
Opens by acknowledging a serious piece of thinking, then immediately identifies its central flaw:
it treats one-third of management as the whole job. The Mintzberg taxonomy is deployed as a
precision tool, not a namecheck, and the reader learns to use it. The risks section names the
incentive problem (post-hoc rationalization) directly, including that Botha sat on the board of
the company that just cut 40% of its workforce. That is a Steve move: name the uncomfortable
inference and let the reader decide. Teaching does not mean blunting that. It closes not with a
verdict but with the actual open question.

## Writing Process Guidance

When drafting a new post for Steve:

1. **Identify what the reader should be able to do afterward.** Not the topic, the capability.
   What thinking tool or distinction do they leave holding? Lead with the tension that makes that
   tool worth acquiring.

2. **Find the entry point.** Is there an external source (book, essay, announcement, data point)
   that sharpens the argument? If so, open there. If not, open on the concrete situation.

3. **Build the section structure.** Each H2 is a claim, not a topic. "What DORA actually
   measures," not "DORA Metrics."

4. **Teach the hard part, do not skip it.** Find the step that is genuinely difficult and build
   the intuition with a concrete case before naming the abstraction. If you find yourself rushing,
   that is the spot to slow down.

5. **Check reconstructability.** Could a smart reader rederive the conclusion from what you gave
   them? If they can only agree, not rebuild, the teaching failed. Add the missing step.

6. **Write declaratively.** First pass: cut every hedge word. Second pass: any sentence over 20
   words, ask whether it should be two.

7. **Close at the frontier.** What does the reader now understand, and where does that
   understanding stop? Put the genuine open question or uncomfortable implication at the end. Do
   not summarize and do not cheerlead.

8. **Edit for voice.** Run the avoid list. Confirm every abstraction has a concrete anchor.
   Confirm the opening does not throat-clear and the "we" earns its place.

## Quick Voice Check

Before finalizing, verify:
- [ ] Opens in the middle of an idea, not a setup
- [ ] The reader leaves able to reconstruct the reasoning, not just agree with it
- [ ] The hardest part was taught, not skipped
- [ ] No em dashes
- [ ] No AI filler
- [ ] Every abstraction has a concrete example within two sentences
- [ ] The argument advances through the sections, does not repeat
- [ ] Critical observations are owned by Steve, not outsourced to "some argue that"
- [ ] No condescension, no flattery, no precious "we"
- [ ] Closing poses a question or marks the edge of understanding, does not summarize or cheerlead
- [ ] Nothing in the post would sound like it came from a content marketing team
